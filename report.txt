Summary and discussion

All five estimation methods ranked the same three sentences lowest in perplexity: "IRAs .", "It was outrageous .", and "The '82 Salon is $ 115 *U* ." Although these examples are short, the results do not establish that shorter sentences generally have lower perplexity. For example, "IRAs ." and "Treasury Securities" both contain two tokens, yet their perplexities differ greatly. Perplexity is computed from the average log probability of the bigram transitions, including sentence boundaries, so a longer sentence does not automatically have a higher score. Unknown-word replacement may affect the rankings, but these results alone do not establish its contribution.

The highest-perplexity rankings show both similarities and differences across methods. Laplace and Dirichlet select the same three examples in the same order: "Treasury Securities", "Municipal Issues", and "Foreign Bond". Jelinek-Mercer and Kneser-Ney have somewhat different rankings, although both include "Treasury Securities" and the sentence beginning "Inventor Claire Marvin". These observations show that the estimation method can change which sentences receive the highest perplexities, even when the lowest-perplexity rankings agree.

MLE differs from the other methods because it assigns zero probability to bigrams that never appeared in the Brown training data. Even if both words appeared individually in training, their combination may be unseen. A single zero-probability bigram makes a sentence's MLE perplexity infinite. Therefore, the three displayed high-perplexity MLE sentences are examples tied at infinity, rather than uniquely the three hardest sentences. Laplace, Dirichlet, Jelinek-Mercer, and Kneser-Ney use smoothing and produce finite perplexities for the examples shown. These selected extremes illustrate differences between the methods, but do not establish which method performs best overall.
